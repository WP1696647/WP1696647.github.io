U.S. federal law focus

01 / The idea

A dot on a map is a clue.

A navigation app may use GPS to estimate where a device is. Time-stamped locations can reveal routines—such as where someone lives, works, worships, or receives care—especially when records are kept and combined.

Ethics asks

What should we do?

Is collecting a precise location necessary for the feature? Did people understand the choice? Is it fair to keep or share the data longer than needed? These are questions about responsibility and harm, even when an action is technically legal.

Law asks

What is legally allowed?

Rules depend on who collects the data, why, where, and which law applies. A legal permission is not automatically an ethical choice—and an ethical concern is not automatically proof of a legal violation.

Keep the boundary clear: the U.S. Supreme Court's Carpenter v. United States decision concerned government access to historical cell-site records under the Fourth Amendment. It did not declare all private app location collection illegal.

Investigators request location records

Answer all three to see your score.

Important: Carpenter is a U.S. federal constitutional decision about government searches for historical cell-site records. It is a focused rule, not a general privacy law for every app, GPS device, or kind of location data.
